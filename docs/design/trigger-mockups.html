Writing a trigger, six ways

How someone tells Ambient what to watch for and what their agent should do, and how each approach pushes them past "notify me" towards flows that do real work.

Today the builder asks for two halves: "When…" (a sentence, matched to kinds of activity and an optional on-device meaning check) and "Then my agent should…". It works, but it frames a trigger as a notification rule, and people who live in chat boxes write one prompt, not a form.

Each approach below keeps Ambient's rules: the decision runs on this device or on a zero-retention model, every trigger is visible, and nothing saves until you've seen what it would have caught on your own recent activity. Each shows the writing screen, the "here's what I understood" state, and how it nudges people towards something bigger.

All names, messages and companies are invented.

The approaches
  1. Just say it · one prompt, read back as chips
  2. Recipe shelf · flows by role, with an ambition ladder
  3. Talk it through · Ambient asks two or three questions
  4. Show me, then hand off · record it once
  5. From your week · suggestions mined from your activity
  6. Standing instructions · a page you write to your assistant
  7. Comparison and recommendation
Approach 1

Just say it

One box, like the composer people already type into all day. Ambient reads the prompt back as a sentence of editable chips, and tests it on your last week before you save.

How it pushes ambition: as you type, "Make it do more" offers the next step using apps it has actually seen you use (Stripe, Slack), and the step list ends in a dashed "Add a step". The coloured underlines show which words became which chip, so long prompts stay legible.

Why

Matches the habit people already have: write one prompt. The structure (watch, only when, then) still exists, but Ambient produces it, and you correct it rather than fill it in. Anything it can't check, like "over a year", is said out loud in coral instead of silently dropped.

Strengths and risks

  • Fastest path from an idea to a saved trigger
  • Invites long, multi-step prompts
  • A misread is easy to miss if people skim the chips
  • Blank box: weak for people with no idea yet

Needs from the engine

  • Prompt → trigger program compiler (kinds, field conditions, NL condition, steps)
  • Span map from words to parts, for the underlines
  • "Can't check" verdicts for conditions with no field

How it pushes ambition: the ladder makes "notify me" visibly the bottom rung. Cards open with three rungs already on, and the next one waits as a dashed outline. Frequency ("~9×/week") comes from your own activity, which tells you how much time the flow gives back.

Why

Most people don't know what proactive agents can do until they see a good one. A shelf of rich recipes sets the bar high, and remixing is less intimidating than a blank page.

Strengths and risks

Needs from the engine

  • "Ready" check: does this machine see these kinds?
  • Frequency estimate from the activity catalog

How it pushes ambition: the middle question is always "how far should it go?", with answers ordered from least to most, and the most ambitious one is the widest button. Counts in the questions ("11 times last week") make the payoff concrete.

Why

Vague intent is the normal starting point ("stay on top of reviews"). Asking back turns it into a precise trigger without the person ever seeing the word "trigger", and the answer options teach what's possible.

Strengths and risks

  • Works from a vague start
  • Each question can carry an example of a bolder answer
  • Slower for people who already know what they want
  • A fourth question would feel like a form in disguise

Needs from the engine

  • A model that picks the most useful next question (on device or zero-retention)
  • Answer options built from the activity catalog
  • Same trigger-program output as approach 1

How it pushes ambition: the recording already contains the whole chore, so the default handoff is "do what I did", not "notify me". The timeline makes chaining across apps the natural unit, without anyone having to describe it.

Why

Some chores are easier to do than to describe. This grows the existing "Show me once" from finding one kind of event into capturing a whole workflow, which is Ambient's unique advantage: it can see the steps.

Strengths and risks

  • No writing at all; works for unfamiliar apps
  • Captures multi-app flows people wouldn't think to describe
  • One recording generalises poorly; needs a second example
  • Agents may not be able to replay every step (no API)

Needs from the engine

  • Recording of a session of kinds, in order, with relevance filtering
  • Turning a recorded sequence into agent instructions
  • Per-step "can the agent do this?" check

How it pushes ambition: the dial opens at "Draft it", not "Tell me", and the replay shows a finished artifact instead of a match list. "One notch further" appears after you've seen the output be good, which is when people say yes to more.

Why

The hardest part is knowing what to automate. Ambient has the evidence, locally. Suggestions that cite your own numbers feel earned rather than salesy, and a replay on last Tuesday is the most convincing backtest there is.

Strengths and risks

  • Zero effort to start; high hit rate
  • Backtest becomes a preview of real output
  • Can feel watched if the tone is wrong
  • Replaying with a real agent costs time and tokens

Needs from the engine

  • Local pattern mining over kinds (frequency, time of day, “X then Y”)
  • Dry-run of an agent on a past event, no side effects
  • Suggestion dismissals remembered

How it pushes ambition: paragraphs invite branching ("if there is…, if not…, if it sounds like…") in a way a single field never does. Seeing several instructions together makes gaps obvious, and the page reads like a job description for your agent, which nudges people to give it more of the job.

Why

People already write standing instructions (CLAUDE.md, a brief to an assistant). This makes triggers feel like delegating, not configuring, and the whole set is visible on one page, which suits Ambient's "always visible" rule.

Strengths and risks

  • Rich branching logic in plain prose
  • All triggers reviewable in one read
  • Editing a paragraph silently changes a live trigger; needs a clear "saved, re-tested" moment
  • Long paragraphs can hide two triggers in one

Needs from the engine

  • Paragraph → trigger program, with branches as steps
  • Diff and re-backtest on edit before it goes live
  • Splitting a paragraph that holds two triggers

Recommendation: build "Just say it" first, as one box with three ways in

Approach 1 is the core every other approach needs anyway: one prompt compiled into a trigger program, read back as editable chips, with a backtest before saving. Build it first, then let the others feed the same box rather than living as separate screens.

From your week (5)+Recipes by role (2)→ fill One prompt box (1)→Reading + backtest→Saved
  1. Now: approach 1 replaces the When/Then sheet. Keep "Show me once" (4) as a chip in the composer, so the event-finding fallback survives. When the reading is unsure, ask one inline question (3) instead of a whole conversation.
  2. Next: empty state. Above the box, show two suggestions from your week (5) once there's enough activity, and role recipes (2) before that. Both just pre-fill the box, so there's one editor to maintain. The ladder's "how far should it go?" becomes the "Make it do more" nudges.
  3. Later: the trigger list becomes the standing-instructions page (6), which needs no new compiler, only edit-and-re-test. Full workflow recording (4b) waits until agents can reliably replay steps across apps.
